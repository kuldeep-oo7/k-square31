The book

Your average is what you bought at, or sold at on a short. Mark is the contract's price at the broker right now, falling back to the exchange close when the market is shut. MTM is the broker's own figure, and it is more than mark minus average: it also holds the profit already banked by partly closing a position. Every figure here is read from GM, not recalculated. Positions are encrypted in this page; the passphrase never leaves your browser.

What the numbers mean

Written out once, because a figure you have to guess at is worse than no figure.

MTM, and why it is not simply mark minus average
A partly closed position has two halves. Say 400 were bought at 100 and 250 of those sold at 104, leaving 150 open against a mark of 105. The closed 250 banked 1,000, the open 150 shows 750, and the broker's MTM of 1,750 is both together. Mark minus average on the open lots alone loses the banked half, so this page reports the broker's figure rather than doing its own arithmetic.
Carry
A futures contract and its share trade at different prices, and that gap must close by expiry. If the future sits above the share you pay that premium away a little each day you hold a long; if it sits below, you are paid to hold. A known cost or credit that happens whatever the price does, shown as what it is worth to your position over one week.
Mark
The contract's latest price, with its source underneath. GM publishes nothing once the market closes, so outside session hours this is the exchange's official close.
Day and month range
Day range is the contract's own high and low for the session. Month range is the share's high and low over the last 21 sessions — the wider context the ladder rungs sit inside.
12-month trend
Where the share has gone over a year, scaled by its own volatility. Buying dips in something that has fallen all year is the largest single source of loss in this rule, so a position leaning against its trend is flagged.
Potential
Most names reach this list because the 2% ladder has a measured record on them. A few arrive the other way: the ladder has none, but every reading together puts the expected move above your 2% floor. Those carry a "potential" tag, offer only the first rung, show no hit rate because there is none to show, and are sized smaller. A 2% floor is a floor — a name set up to travel further belongs here even when the slice-harvesting rule cannot measure it.
Traded before
The name appears in the journal exported from the old platform. A marker, not a filter: 28 of those names have since left the F&O segment, and 114 tradable names were never in it.
Ladder
Whether the 2% weekly rule has a measured edge on that name over five years. "No measured edge" is not a forecast of loss — it means the rule that opened the position has no historical support in that name.

Rule measured: the week's first open is the anchor; orders rest 2% apart; a leg exits on the first touch of a 2% gain. Costs charged at 0.06% per round trip. Hit rates carry 95% Wilson bounds and each rung clears a t-statistic hurdle raised for testing five rungs. Reported weeks are out of sample.

Gates: a 12-month trend running against the ladder is refused outright, as is a results date inside the trading week. A measured edge is not a promise.
